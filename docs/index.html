Why doesn’t an intelligent person correct themselves?

An answer you can follow through a calculation.

One surgeon, one day

At nine in the morning she is in an operating theatre. A junior colleague tells her that her incision is wrong. She stops, looks, says “you are right,” and changes what she is doing.

Six hours later her husband says almost the same words: she is being unfair to their daughter. She does not stop. She explains why he is mistaken, then why he has always been mistaken, and the evening ends where such evenings end.

Nothing about her intelligence changed between the two scenes. Nothing about her capacity for effortful thought changed. What changed was whether the truth was allowed in.

The usual explanations do not survive this. She is not stubborn, or she would have been stubborn in the morning. She is certainly not slow. And these are not two different people; it is the same woman, six hours apart.

What follows proposes that between noticing and acting there is something rarely treated as a thing of its own: whether a truth about oneself is let in. Not whether it is understood. Whether it is allowed to be true of you.

Three conditions, and it hangs on all three

For a person to act against their strongest habit, three things must hold at once. They are not stages traversed in order, but requirements that have to be met together. Each one sets a ceiling on the outcome.

Condition 1
Noticing
The discrepancy has to arrive at all, and strongly enough to argue against what was expected.
Well studied: error signals in the brain, the ERN and the Pe.
Condition 2
Acceptingthe contribution
It has to be allowed to hold of oneself. Not understood, not conceded in general, but in this case, about this person, now.
The building blocks exist: what self-worth is staked on, and what it costs to be struck there.
Condition 3
Overriding
The new intention has to outvote the practised one. That has a price, and the price is not always worth paying.
Well studied: the expected value of control.
What is new here, and what is not. All three building blocks are established and studied separately. What is claimed as new is the wiring: that the middle condition is one of its own, that it does not reduce to understanding, and that the three cannot substitute for one another.

The objection

It is the obvious one: this is just another word for intelligence. People who can do more correct themselves more often. If that holds, raising ability should shrink the difference between morning and afternoon. Let us look.

What just happened

Her ability worked in both scenes, and by exactly the same factor. That is not an approximation: acceptance cancels out of the ratio, because the slider never touches it. The gain is necessarily identical in both.

And yet she ends up high on one side and near the floor on the other. Nothing on the right is blocked and nothing is broken. That value rose too, by the same factor. It rose under a ceiling.

That is the whole claim. Ability is not a shortcut past this point. It multiplies whatever gets through. When little gets through, it multiplies little.

From which something testable follows. A highly able person whose self-worth is staked on a domain should collapse there instead of spending ability as compensation. An account in which the three conditions add up predicts the opposite: that the large ability makes up for the small acceptance.

One caution about what you just watched, because it matters. The factor is the same on both sides by construction, since the slider does not touch acceptance, and a constant factor of that kind can be produced by rescaling; on its own it decides nothing. What the manuscript puts forward for testing is the absolute gain, the actual distance between the two numbers at the bottom of each scene. On the left it is large, on the right it is almost nothing. Deciding between the two accounts also needs the rival fitted to the same data, and that is a study, not a slider.

The calculation

The core is a product. Three conditions multiplied rather than added, so that none can replace another:

from math import exp

def sigmoid(x):
    return 1 / (1 + exp(-x))

def autonomy(detection, acceptance, override):
    """How far a person can act against their strongest schema."""
    return detection * acceptance * override

And here is the case the manuscript works through: an able man with an excellent error detector and formidable modelling ability, whose self-worth is staked on exactly this domain.

>>> round(autonomy(sigmoid(2), sigmoid(-3), sigmoid(2)), 4)
0.0368
# Both faculties removed, the gate still shut:
>>> round(autonomy(0.5, sigmoid(-3), 0.5), 4)
0.0119

# His two great faculties tripled the result.
# It is still three hundredths.

These figures come from the full logistic values, not from rounded intermediates; chaining rounded numbers is itself a way to get a worked example wrong. Units are arbitrary and illustrative: they demonstrate an ordering, not a measurement, and none of them should be quoted as measured values. The same holds for the figures in the display above.

What would refute this

The manuscript names three forms the test can take and three patterns that would sink the model. The one it leans on: where acceptance is low, the effect of forecasting depth on the outcome should fall below what a study of that size could detect, rather than merely shrink. If it merely shrinks, the conditions are trading off, and the architecture is wrong.

Before any of that, one thing has to hold that is easy to overlook. The three conditions must be measurable separately, without using the outcome to do it. If acceptance is recognised by the fact that someone changed their behaviour, nothing has been explained, only renamed. That is a question about measurement rather than about the model, and the manuscript treats it in its own appendix.

Where this comes from

This page accompanies The Acceptance Gate: Three Necessary Conditions for the Capacity to Act Against One’s Strongest Schema by Nevzat Subasioglu, independent researcher, Switzerland. The manuscript carries the derivation, the demarcation against existing models, and a proposal for how the matter could be settled experimentally.

The question worth asking about a person is not how clever they are or how hard they try, but a third one we have not been asking: what would it cost them to be wrong?
